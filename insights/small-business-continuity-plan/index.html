Business Continuity Planning for Australian Clinics and Salons

Prepare a clinic continuity plan for people, bookings, business data, suppliers, customer communication and recovery.

A local power outage, storm, flood, heat event, illness, cyber incident, equipment failure or building problem can interrupt a small service business with little warning. When the owner is also the receptionist, bookkeeper, practitioner and system administrator, an absence can make ordinary decisions difficult. A basic continuity plan helps the team protect people, communicate clearly and restart essential work in a sensible order.

Australian businesses face different hazards depending on location, building, services and suppliers. This guide is a starting point for operational planning, not emergency, insurance, legal or financial advice. Follow emergency service instructions and local government alerts. The Australian Government’s business guidance provides current steps for preparing for emergencies and developing a plan. Check those sources and consult qualified professionals for your business circumstances.

Decide what the business must protect first

Start with people. List staff, contractors, customers who may be on site, people with access needs and emergency contacts. Explain how to raise an alarm, where to go, who calls emergency services and who checks that people have left safely. Do not place property, stock or client appointments ahead of life and health.

Then identify the essential services and information needed to operate. A salon may need booking access, customer contact, payment capability, product supply and a safe water supply. A tattoo studio may need secure appointment and artwork records, suitable equipment and a way to contact clients if the premises close. A mobile beauty business may need vehicle access, power, travel routes and a safe alternative location. The actual priorities differ by business.

Set a realistic recovery priority. What must be restored within hours, a day, a week or longer? A business might be able to take bookings while unable to deliver services, or process payments using an approved alternative when the internet fails. Think through these scenarios in advance. Write down a minimum viable way to operate safely and the conditions that require the business to pause.

Identify likely disruptions in your area

Use local knowledge and official information to identify hazards. Check local flood, bushfire, storm, heat, transport, power and water risks. Ask the landlord or property manager about building procedures, backup power, access arrangements and insurance responsibilities. A coastal business, a regional studio and a city-centre salon may face different disruptions. Avoid copying another business’s plan without checking local conditions.

Map dependencies. These can include staff, electricity, internet, booking and payment providers, suppliers, delivery routes, equipment maintenance, building access, cleaning contractors, utilities and external advisers. For each dependency, note what happens if it is unavailable and who can provide an update. A single supplier or one person with the only administrator password can become a bottleneck.

Assess duration and impact. A 30-minute outage during a quiet period differs from a three-day closure during a busy week. Consider damage to equipment, missed appointments, lost income, stock spoilage, delayed wages or supplier payments, customer data access and staff wellbeing. The purpose is not to predict every event but to identify a handful of plausible disruptions and practical responses.

Protect customer records and digital access

Keep important business records backed up and know how to restore them. Confirm the booking provider’s recovery process, export options, support channel and data retention if the account is unavailable. Do not assume that a cloud service automatically gives you an independent backup. Test restoration with a safe sample and keep emergency support contacts outside the system that may fail.

Protect administrator access. Use unique passwords and multi-factor authentication, record who has access and maintain a secure recovery route. If the owner becomes unavailable, the business may need an authorised backup person. Share access through approved account roles or a password manager, not by writing a password on a noticeboard. Revoke access promptly when a person leaves or changes roles.

Decide how to contact customers without exposing personal information. A contact list should be stored securely, accessed only by authorised people and used for the defined operational purpose. If a closure notice is sent by email or SMS, keep it factual and follow applicable communication and privacy requirements. Avoid using an emergency as a reason to send unrelated promotions.

Plan communication before a disruption

Write a short contact tree: who contacts emergency services, who updates staff, who speaks with the landlord, who contacts suppliers, who informs customers and who communicates with insurers, accountants or lenders. Identify a primary and backup person. Keep current phone numbers and an offline copy in a secure place. A digital plan is less useful if its only copy is behind a login that cannot be reached.

Prepare message templates for common situations such as a temporary closure, appointment rescheduling, loss of phone or internet access, and reopening. Keep the language accurate and calm. Do not promise a reopening time before it is confirmed. Give customers a clear next step and update the message when circumstances change. For a public incident, appoint one spokesperson to reduce conflicting information.

Choose the communication channels customers already use, and verify that the business still controls them. A social-media-only plan may fail if the account is locked or customers do not follow it. A phone tree may not reach people whose numbers are outdated. Keep more than one route and test how it works. Do not publish private customer details in a public post.

Protect people, equipment and stock

Check that staff know evacuation routes, assembly points, emergency contacts and how to stop work safely. Consider customers who are on site, mobility needs, language needs and people who may not know the building. Coordinate the plan with the property owner and other occupants. Practise at a suitable time and improve the plan after the exercise.

Secure equipment and hazardous products according to their instructions and local requirements. Do not ask staff to enter an unsafe building to rescue stock or retrieve a laptop. Make a list of critical equipment and service contacts, including serial numbers, warranties and maintenance arrangements where useful. Store evidence of ownership and insurance securely.

Plan for stock interruption. Identify critical supplies, safe approved substitutes and supplier alternatives. Keep a sensible buffer where the cost and shelf life justify it. A disruption plan should not encourage unsafe substitutions or expired product use. If an essential item cannot be sourced, reschedule or stop the service rather than risking customer safety.

Understand insurance and financial dependencies

Review relevant business insurance with an appropriately qualified broker or adviser. Confirm what cover exists, what exclusions or conditions apply, the notification process, waiting periods, business interruption terms and what evidence may be needed. Do not assume a policy covers every event or all lost income. Keep current policy documents and broker contacts accessible.

List critical payment dates and discuss the options available if the business experiences a closure. A business may need to consider wages, rent, loan repayments, supplier obligations, refunds, deposits and customer communications. Decisions depend on contracts and law. Speak with an accountant, lender, insurer or lawyer rather than making assumptions based on an old plan or social media advice.

Keep emergency spending authority clear. Who may arrange a repair, temporary site, replacement device or customer refund? Set limits and documentation requirements. A sole trader may need a trusted adviser who can help identify urgent contacts if the owner is ill. In a larger studio, an authorised manager may need a way to approve essential operations without access to unrelated financial information.

Make a simple recovery playbook

For each priority scenario, list immediate actions, decision owner, contact route, safe operating limits and recovery steps. A power outage playbook could cover checking safety, contacting the utility, securing equipment, deciding which appointments can continue, messaging customers and checking systems before reopening. A cyber incident requires different steps, including provider and bank contacts, evidence preservation and specialist advice.

Separate response from recovery. The immediate response protects people and prevents further harm. Recovery restores bookings, equipment, customer service, records and normal operations. Owners can overlook the recovery stage and reopen before verifying that devices, payments, stock or data are safe. Define a checklist for returning to service and who confirms each item.

After an incident or practice exercise, record what worked, what failed and what should change. Update the plan and tell the people who need it. A plan that no one knows about is only paperwork. Keep it short enough to use, detailed enough to guide decisions and current enough to trust.

A 30-day continuity setup

In week one, list essential people, services, systems, suppliers and records. Identify the most likely local disruptions and read official emergency guidance. In week two, confirm how to contact staff, customers, the landlord, providers, insurers and advisers if the usual phone or internet fails.

In week three, check backups and access recovery, identify safe alternatives for key supplies and prepare closure and rescheduling messages. In week four, run a brief scenario with the team. Ask what would happen in the first hour, who would make each decision and how the business would reopen. Record the gaps, assign owners and schedule the next review.

Build scenarios around the first hour and the next week

Write one scenario for an immediate safety event and one for a prolonged business interruption. In the first hour, the key decisions may include whether to evacuate, stop appointments, contact emergency services, secure the premises and account for staff and customers. During the following week, the business may need to arrange another location, reach booked customers, handle deposits and payments, access records, communicate with staff and protect income.

For each scenario, write the trigger for activating the plan, the person with authority, the initial actions, the communication route and the condition for reopening. If the owner is unavailable, name a backup decision-maker and describe their authority. Keep responsibilities realistic and confirm that the person agrees to the role. A plan that assigns every task to the same busy owner may fail at the exact moment it is needed.

Separate information that is safe to share publicly from private business details. A public closure notice should not reveal security arrangements, personal contact information or customer appointment lists. The emergency contact sheet should be available to authorised staff and kept current. Store a copy offline or in a secure place accessible during a power or internet outage.

Plan for supplier and location disruption

List essential suppliers and alternatives: product distributors, equipment repair, utilities, payment services, internet, cleaning, waste collection, landlord, and any specialist contractors. Record order minimums, typical delivery timing, emergency support and contractual commitments. Ask suppliers how they handle a regional outage or a product recall. Do not assume an alternative is available during a widespread event.

For a temporary closure, decide whether work can move to another suitable location, be rescheduled or be paused. Consider practitioner licensing, insurance, equipment, customer privacy and local permissions before moving services. Do not use a borrowed room or home location without confirming that it is safe and permitted. If service continuity is not possible, communicate clearly and handle customer money and bookings according to contract and law.

A temporary site may change the customer journey. Confirm access, parking, opening hours, payment tools, hygiene facilities and who will meet customers. Provide directions through a trusted channel and update online listings where needed. Ensure no booking page continues directing clients to a closed site without a clear alert. Reopen only when the environment and equipment are ready.

Protect financial and operational evidence

Keep copies of insurance details, leases, equipment invoices, supplier contracts, payroll contacts and key business records in an approved, secure location. Store enough information to support claims and recovery, but avoid duplicating sensitive data without reason. Make a list of where originals live, who can access them and how they are backed up.

After damage or an incident, follow insurer and authority instructions before disposing of equipment or altering the site when safe to do so. Photograph damage only when it is safe and permitted. Keep records of expenses, closures, rescheduled work and communications. Ask the insurer or adviser what evidence they need; do not assume that a generic checklist satisfies a policy condition.

Maintain a simple operating cash view as part of continuity planning. Identify fixed commitments, expected settlement timing and any reserve or facility the business may be able to use. A continuity event can create costs before any insurance payment arrives. Speak with an accountant, insurer and lender about arrangements in advance, and do not assume a claim or payment is guaranteed.

Support staff and customers during recovery

A closure can affect employee income, schedules and wellbeing as well as customers. Tell workers what is known, what remains uncertain, when the next update will arrive and who can answer questions. Seek employment advice about shifts, leave, pay and other obligations. Do not make promises based on a hoped-for reopening date.

Prioritise customers with imminent appointments and those who need time-sensitive communication. Use a consistent process to offer rescheduling, refunds or other options in line with applicable terms and law. Keep records of decisions so different staff do not offer inconsistent answers. If the disruption involves client data or a cyber incident, seek privacy and legal advice before communicating details.

Recovery can take longer than the immediate emergency. Set a regular update rhythm, even if the update is that there is no new information. Review which customers need direct contact and which can receive a public notice. Protect staff from being expected to answer personal accounts at all hours. Clear roles and boundaries improve the quality of communication.

Learn from exercises and real disruptions

A short tabletop exercise can expose missing details without putting people or systems at risk. Ask the team to imagine a severe storm warning before opening. Who checks official alerts? Who stops new bookings? How are customers notified? Where is the staff contact list? Which supplier or landlord is called? What if the internet fails too? Record unanswered questions rather than trying to solve everything on the spot.

After a real disruption, hold a review when people are safe and ready. Ask what protected customers and staff, which steps were confusing, what information was missing and where the plan relied on one person. Assign changes and due dates. Do not focus only on whether the business reopened quickly; consider safety, customer fairness and employee wellbeing.

Update the plan whenever the business moves, changes systems, adds services, changes suppliers or loses a key team member. Review contacts periodically and after staff changes. Store the date of last review on the document. An outdated emergency contact or old booking link can make a good plan unusable.\n## Set recovery priorities by service and system

Create a simple priority table: activity, maximum tolerable interruption, dependencies, minimum safe alternative and decision owner. The table helps distinguish “must restore immediately” from “can wait until tomorrow.” For example, emergency customer communications may be urgent while a non-critical social post can wait. Payroll and appointment systems may have different recovery expectations.

Ask the team and key suppliers to review the priorities. An owner may think that a particular report is essential while staff know the business can operate safely without it for several days. Conversely, a practitioner may depend on an approved record that the owner did not realise was critical. The process should reflect real work and comply with privacy, safety and professional requirements.

Avoid writing recovery targets that cannot be met. If the booking provider cannot restore data within the desired time, identify a fallback or select another tool when contracts are reviewed. If the only trained person for a task is away, cross-training may be needed. The gap itself is useful information and can guide investment before an emergency.

Consider continuity in digital and physical investments

When buying software, ask how the vendor handles outages, what export is available, how quickly support responds and how a customer can contact the business if the platform fails. When purchasing equipment, check repair support, warranty, consumables and whether another unit can provide a safe backup. When signing a lease, clarify building access, emergency procedures, insurance responsibilities and service interruption terms with a qualified adviser.

A business does not need to duplicate every system or maintain costly spare equipment. It should understand which single points of failure have serious consequences and decide whether to mitigate, accept or transfer the risk. Write the decision and review it after a change. Insurance may transfer some financial impact but does not restore records or customer trust by itself.

Reopen only when the conditions are safe

Define who confirms that people can return, equipment is safe, utilities are functioning, the site is accessible and records or payments are reliable. Follow emergency services, building managers, utility providers and qualified technical advice. Do not reopen because the booking diary is full if the environment has not been checked.

Tell staff what is known and provide a way to raise a concern without pressure. If a system was compromised, have the appropriate specialist confirm recovery before reconnecting it. If the disruption affected customer records, seek privacy advice about notification duties. A careful reopening protects the business from a second incident and helps staff feel confident returning to work.

What matters most

A continuity plan protects people first, helps the business communicate and gives the team an order for restoring essential work. It should reflect local hazards, real system dependencies and the people who can act, not a generic template alone.

Keep contact information and backups accessible, practise a realistic scenario and review the plan when systems, staff or premises change. Follow official emergency instructions during a real event. Ask qualified insurance, financial, legal, safety and cyber professionals for advice where the business needs it.
