A Weekly Cash Flow Routine for Australian Clinics and Salons

Build a practical weekly cash routine for Australian clinics using expected income, planned costs and realistic assumptions.

A profitable-looking month can still feel tight when supplier bills, wages, rent and tax obligations arrive before customer payments clear. That is the difference between a result recorded over a period and the timing of cash available on a particular day. Owners of appointment-based businesses feel this quickly: a cancelled week, equipment repair or quiet period can affect the bank balance before the monthly accounts explain why.

A weekly cash routine helps an owner see timing earlier and ask better questions. It is not a forecast of guaranteed sales and it does not replace bookkeeping, tax advice or financial planning. The examples here are illustrative. Confirm the accounting treatment, tax obligations and payment arrangements that apply to your own business with an Australian accountant or bookkeeper.

Cash flow is about timing, not just profit

Profit is normally measured using accounting rules over a reporting period. Cash flow records money moving into or out of accounts. A business can record a sale before cash reaches the bank, or pay for stock before the products are used. Loan principal, owner drawings, equipment purchases and tax payments can affect cash differently from profit. The distinction matters because an owner cannot pay a supplier with a profit figure sitting in a report.

List the timing of the ordinary business cycle. A client books, pays a deposit or settles after an appointment, the payment provider batches funds, the bank receives the deposit, and transaction fees may be deducted. Meanwhile, wages, rent, utilities, suppliers and software have their own due dates. Map the real dates and settlement delays instead of assuming all sales arrive instantly. When the calendar is visible, owners can often spot a timing mismatch before it becomes a crisis.

Separate business and personal money where possible, and reconcile business transactions regularly. Mixing accounts makes it harder to understand whether a cost belongs to the business and complicates reporting. The Australian Taxation Office publishes guidance on business records and the information businesses should keep. Your bookkeeper can help align the weekly cash view with the accounting system so totals are not copied inconsistently.

Start with opening cash and dated movements

A simple short-range view begins with the cleared opening bank balance on a known date. Add expected cash receipts on the dates they are reasonably expected to settle. Subtract planned payments on the due dates. The result is an estimated closing balance. Repeat the calculation for each week in the planning horizon. A rolling thirteen-week view is common in many businesses because it provides time to notice near-term pressure, but it is only useful if owners update it and understand its assumptions.

Use dates, not vague labels such as “sales next month.” For bookings, account for deposits, settlement timing, cancellations and refunds. For invoices, distinguish issued invoices from amounts actually likely to arrive. If a customer is late paying, a single optimistic date can make the chart falsely reassuring. Mark uncertain receipts as uncertain and create a conservative scenario where they arrive later or not at all during the period.

Do not count the same money twice. A booking deposit may already be part of an expected receipt; the final appointment payment is only the remaining amount. If a payment processor reports gross sales and deducts fees before settlement, choose a consistent treatment: show gross inflow and fee outflow separately, or show the net settlement with a note. Either approach can work for planning as long as the assumptions are clear and reconciled to the bank.

Build realistic inflow assumptions

For each cash source, write down what evidence supports the date and amount. A confirmed appointment with a deposit is different from a general expectation based on last month. A recurring client invoice is different from a quote that has not been accepted. Treat each class differently. Estimate cash from completed bookings, product sales, approved finance or grants, insurance claims and refunds according to their actual status. Avoid including funding or partner support before it is formally approved and the timing is confirmed.

Use recent history as a reference, not a promise. Compare several weeks or months and note seasonal events, school holidays, staff leave, weather disruption and local factors. A hair salon may see a pattern around graduation or holiday periods; a tattoo studio may have projects booked long in advance; a mobile beauty business may be affected by travel and weather. Past averages can help shape an assumption but cannot confirm what customers will do next.

When uncertainty is high, use scenarios. A base view can reflect current bookings and usual payment patterns. A cautious view can assume a lower level of new bookings or slower payments. A growth view can include a planned event or new service, but clearly identify the extra assumptions and costs. Compare the closing cash and the date of the lowest point. This is more informative than presenting one confident-looking sales number.

Map outflows that are easy to forget

Owners usually remember rent and wages but may miss irregular costs. Add supplier orders, equipment maintenance, insurance renewals, licences, subscriptions, payment fees, loan repayments, training, superannuation, tax or GST payments as advised by a qualified professional, refunds and planned purchases. Note the payment date and whether the amount changes with trading. A yearly insurance renewal may create a cash dip even when the cost is spread across formal accounting reports.

Put tax and payroll dates on the calendar

Do not treat every dollar in the bank as available to spend, but also do not use a universal percentage as a substitute for tax advice. GST treatment depends on the type of supply and the circumstances. The ATO explains that some health services can be GST-free when the relevant conditions are met, while other services may be taxable. Beauty, cosmetic and wellness businesses can offer a mixture of services, so do not assume that every treatment has the same GST treatment. Ask a registered tax professional how the rules apply to your actual services and records.

Add known lodgement and payment dates to the cash calendar once your tax or bookkeeping adviser confirms them. Keep the amount as an estimate until it is supported by the business’s records. Review it when sales, purchases, credits or service mix changes. The point is to surface timing and questions early, not to calculate a liability from a generic blog formula.

If you employ eligible workers, include super in the payroll schedule as well. As at November 2026, the ATO says the super guarantee rate is 12% of qualifying earnings, and Payday Super applies from 1 July 2026. Employers generally need contributions received by the employee’s fund within seven business days of payday, subject to applicable exceptions and transition rules. Check the current ATO instructions and payroll provider setup rather than carrying forward a quarterly schedule from an older cash plan.

For stock, link purchases to actual usage and supplier lead times. Ordering too much consumes cash and storage space; ordering too late can create a service interruption. For technology, write down annual renewals and any automatic price changes. For equipment, distinguish a planned replacement from an unexpected repair. Keep personal purchases out of business cash projections and make owner drawings visible so the business does not treat them as invisible.

Check who controls each payment and how it is approved. A recurring debit for a tool no longer used can quietly increase costs. A legitimate supplier invoice with changed bank details needs independent verification before payment. Cash planning is also a chance to find process risks: multiple cards, unreviewed direct debits, unclear approval thresholds and no backup person to access accounts can harm operations during leave or an emergency.

Choose a rhythm that fits a small team

A weekly review can take less than half an hour when records are organised. Pick the same day each week. Update the cleared bank balance, mark which expected payments actually arrived, confirm upcoming bills, check booking cancellations and revise assumptions. Record the date of the update, who made it and any unresolved items. The aim is not accounting perfection every morning; it is a repeated habit that catches changes in time to act.

A monthly review has a different purpose. Compare the forecast with actual movements, identify recurring errors and adjust assumptions. If you expected a supplier bill in one week but it arrived in another, update the expected timing. If a class of customers regularly pays later than planned, use observed settlement behaviour. If a sales assumption was too optimistic, explain why rather than simply resetting the number until the chart looks good.

Assign a backup owner. In a sole-trader business, this may be an accountant or trusted authorised person who can help during illness or leave. In a studio with staff, document where invoices are received, how approvals work and what to do if a payment fails. Keep access secure and follow provider permissions. Do not share a bank password in a spreadsheet or informal team chat.

Read the warning signals and respond early

A falling projected closing balance is a prompt to investigate, not proof that the business is failing. Check whether a large receipt is late, a payment is duplicated, an expected outflow was omitted, or the schedule changed. Find the earliest week where the cash view becomes uncomfortable. The earlier the gap appears, the more options the owner may have to manage timing responsibly.

Review discretionary purchases against the business plan. Ask suppliers about payment timing before a bill is overdue. Contact a lender early if the business has a facility and needs to discuss terms; do not assume an extension is guaranteed. Check the accuracy of invoices and outstanding receivables. If customer payment reminders are used, make them professional and consistent. Consider a deposit policy only after examining customer expectations, legal requirements and the effect on conversion.

Do not use a credit card, tax reserve or client deposit to disguise an underlying loss without professional advice. Borrowing can bridge a timing issue, but it can also increase future obligations. If the shortfall is structural, review pricing, costs, capacity, sales mix and owner withdrawals with an accountant or financial adviser. Australia’s business.gov.au finance guidance provides starting points for finding support; eligibility and available programs change, so verify details with the official provider.

Example: two versions of a busy month

Suppose a fictional studio has $18,000 in cleared cash on the first day of the month. It expects $26,000 from appointments, but some payments settle later. It has $20,000 of dated costs including wages, rent, supplies and bills. A simple month-end estimate appears to leave $24,000. That total can conceal a week where $10,000 of supplier and payroll payments leave before the appointment receipts settle. The business might be positive at the end of the month and still need a plan for the earlier date.

A conservative view could assume that a portion of new appointments do not book and a large customer invoice arrives two weeks later than expected. Then the owner can see whether the temporary low point is manageable. The appropriate response depends on cash available, commitments, credit terms, business structure and professional advice. It may involve adjusting the timing of optional purchases, following up a legitimate invoice or discussing a facility in advance. It should not involve misleading a supplier or using money that belongs to clients without considering obligations.

The lesson is to focus on the lowest point and the sequence of movements. The closing figure alone does not show whether obligations can be met as they fall due. If a business regularly faces a narrow buffer, the owner should discuss a suitable reserve, working-capital approach and risk plan with a qualified adviser. There is no single reserve target for every salon or service business.

Keep the cash view connected to operations

Appointment capacity affects cash, but do not treat every open slot as a guaranteed sale. A team can compare future booked hours, expected average payment timing and cancellation history with prior periods. This helps ask operational questions: are reminders working, are deposits handled consistently, is a popular service under-booked, or does the business need to protect time for consultations and clean-down? Any marketing follow-up must comply with applicable consent and privacy requirements.

Stock and equipment decisions also connect to cash. A low price per unit may still be a poor deal if the business must hold too much stock, waste expired products or tie up money before demand is clear. Compare total cost, delivery lead time, warranty and consumables. For a major purchase, review available cash scenarios and possible financing terms with a professional before committing.

Staff leave and owner wellbeing belong in the plan too. A business dependent on one practitioner may see sales change during absence, while wages and rent continue. Cross-training, documented routines and realistic capacity planning can increase resilience. Avoid forcing staff to fill every hour as a cash response if it increases fatigue or risks service quality. Sustainable operations protect customer trust and long-term capacity.

Common forecasting errors

One common error is treating the booking diary as cash already received. Appointments can move, cancel, refund or fail to settle as expected. Another is treating invoice revenue as a confirmed bank deposit. A third is including GST as if it were available working capital without checking the business’s accounting and tax treatment. Keep the projection aligned with a qualified bookkeeper’s advice and the way receipts actually reach the bank.

Owners also forget owner drawings, debt repayments, annual renewals or one-off equipment purchases. They may use a growth forecast that quietly assumes more staff hours or stock but records only the extra sales. Build the cost and capacity side alongside the opportunity. If a planned marketing campaign succeeds, the business may need product, contractor or room capacity before the income arrives.

Finally, a forecast can become stale if no one compares it with actuals. An old projection is a document, not a management tool. Use a simple format the owner can update, show uncertainty honestly and investigate differences without blame. The routine should give people a better view, not create a second accounting system that takes more time than it saves.

A 30-day cash visibility setup

In week one, identify the bank accounts, payment processors, accounting records and recurring bills that should inform the view. Reconcile the opening balance to cleared funds. Create a list of payments with due dates and a list of expected receipts with a confidence level. Avoid storing passwords or unnecessary client details in the file.

In week two, build a short weekly forecast and have the bookkeeper review the structure. Confirm how the business should treat GST, wages, super, tax and loans. Add known irregular items. In week three, run the regular update and write down which receipts or outflows differed from expectations. Mark why and change the assumption only when evidence supports doing so.

In week four, compare the projection with bank activity and pick one operational question. Perhaps the studio should adjust the timing of a planned purchase, review overdue invoices, or understand settlement delays. Make one decision, assign an owner and set a review date. If the business is already unable to meet commitments, contact an accountant or qualified adviser promptly rather than relying on a template.

Create a reserve policy rather than a lucky balance

A cash reserve is money deliberately kept available for ordinary volatility and disruption. There is no universal number of weeks that fits every business. A single-chair barber with low fixed costs differs from a multi-room salon with employees, equipment finance and seasonal demand. Build a reserve target from the business’s fixed commitments, payment timing, income variability, insurance and recovery needs with an accountant or financial adviser.

Separate reserve money in the records and define when it may be used. A planned equipment replacement, quiet month or emergency closure may each have different criteria. A reserve is not a substitute for maintaining tax obligations or meeting payroll. If the business draws from it, document the reason and a plan to replenish it. This gives the owner a more honest view of whether an apparent cash surplus is actually available for expansion.

Review the reserve after changes in rent, staffing, debt, opening hours or service mix. Growth can increase cash pressure before it increases settled receipts: more bookings may require stock, wages, training or contractor payments first. A busy campaign may therefore create a temporary cash need. Model the timing, not just the expected sales. Discuss a major expansion with qualified advisers before signing a lease or financing equipment.

What matters most

Cash flow improves when the owner understands both the amount and timing of money entering and leaving the business. A weekly, dated view can reveal a pinch point early enough to investigate options. It cannot guarantee future sales, determine tax obligations or fix an unprofitable service model by itself.

Start from cleared cash, use evidence-based receipts and complete outflows, review the lowest point, and compare forecasts with actual bank movements. Keep business and personal records clear, protect access, and ask a qualified Australian adviser about accounting, tax, debt and financing decisions. A small, honest forecast that gets updated is more useful than a detailed projection no one trusts.
