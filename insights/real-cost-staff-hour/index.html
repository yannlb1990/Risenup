The Real Cost of a Staff Hour: A Practical Guide for Australian Clinics

Understand what a staff hour really costs your clinic, from award pay and super to productive time, allowances and roster design.

Why the hourly rate is only the starting point

When an owner asks what a staff member costs per hour, the first answer is usually the rate on the payslip. That rate matters, but it describes only part of the cost of having a person available to deliver services. A useful business view also considers employer super, award-specific allowances, paid leave, training, payroll administration, workers compensation, recruitment, and the time the employee is paid but not available for a booked client.

This broader view is not a judgement about employees. It is a way to make pricing and roster decisions using a more honest denominator. People are not interchangeable units of labour, and a cost calculation cannot capture skill, care, continuity, safety or the quality of a client experience. It can help an owner understand whether the business has enough revenue and productive capacity to support the way work is arranged.

Use the model in this guide as a management estimate. It is not a payroll calculator, award interpretation, employment forecast or recommended wage target. The correct minimum conditions depend on the employee’s role, classification, age, employment type, hours, applicable award or agreement, state or territory and current rules. Check the current award and obtain qualified payroll or employment advice when anything is uncertain.

Start with the correct employment settings

Before calculating anything, confirm the employee’s actual role and working arrangement. A clinic might employ receptionists, hairdressers, beauty therapists, managers, apprentices or people whose work spans several duties. The correct award and classification may depend on the work performed, not only the job title an owner has given it.

The Fair Work Ombudsman’s Paying Employees resources explain minimum pay, award rates, penalty rates, allowances, payslips and record keeping. For businesses covered by the Hair and Beauty Industry Award, use the current award and Fair Work tools to check the relevant classification and conditions. Do not copy last year’s pay guide into a spreadsheet and assume it remains correct after an annual review.

Also confirm whether the worker is an employee or a genuine independent contractor. The label in an agreement or invoice does not by itself settle the legal relationship. If classification is unclear, ask an employment adviser or lawyer before using a contractor rate as a substitute for employee costs. Incorrect classification can create unpaid entitlements and other liabilities.

Record the basis of your calculation: role, classification, employment type, date checked, source and who checked it. That small discipline makes later reviews much easier, especially when roles change or minimum conditions are updated.

Build the cost in layers

Keep the calculation readable by separating items instead of hiding them in one percentage. A working sheet can show:

  • Ordinary pay: the current rate for the employee’s hours and classification, based on the applicable award, agreement or contract.
  • Penalty rates and allowances: amounts that apply because of the hours worked, duties performed or conditions of the work. These can vary by day, time, role and circumstances.
  • Employer super: super guarantee on the employee’s qualifying earnings. As at November 2026, the ATO lists a 12% rate. Payday Super started on 1 July 2026. In general, contributions need to be received by the employee’s super fund within seven business days after payday, unless an extended timeframe applies, such as for new employees. Check the ATO’s current guidance before changing payroll settings.
  • Paid leave and leave loading: account for the paid entitlements that apply to the employee and check whether the award or agreement provides a loading or other payment. Avoid adding a generic allowance without confirming the applicable conditions.
  • Workers compensation and payroll-related charges: these vary by jurisdiction, business activity, wages and eligibility. Use the relevant state or territory authority or adviser for the correct treatment.
  • Training, recruitment and administration: onboarding, required training, payroll setup, uniforms or other role-specific costs may matter for a particular business. Record them separately so owners can tell recurring employment costs from one-off setup costs.

Do not assume every item is calculated on the same base. Super rules have their own definition of qualifying earnings; leave, allowances and penalties follow their own instruments; state charges have separate rules. A business model should be a clear planning view, not a substitute for payroll calculations.

A small example: paid hours versus service hours

Suppose a fictional employee has an ordinary hourly pay rate of $35. If the employee’s entire $35 for one hour is qualifying earnings, a simple 12% super illustration adds $4.20. The example therefore has $39.20 in direct pay and super for that hour, before considering any leave-related cost, applicable loading, penalties, allowances, insurance, recruitment, training or administration.

Now suppose the employee is paid for 38 hours in a week, while the roster shows 30 hours available for client services. Using only the $39.20 direct-pay example, the weekly direct pay and super estimate is $1,489.60. Dividing this by 30 service-available hours gives about $49.65 per service-available hour. This is a narrow planning illustration, not the employee’s legal hourly rate and not the cost of a completed treatment. It excludes other employer costs and assumes a particular set of hours and qualifying earnings.

The calculation helps show why comparing the payslip rate directly with a service price can mislead. The business pays for the wider work pattern, not only the minutes spent with a client. At the same time, not every non-client hour is waste. Consultation, clean-down, stock checks, team meetings, training and care-related records can be necessary parts of a good service.

The question is not “How do I make every paid minute billable?” It is “Do our prices, service mix and operating routines account for the work our team needs to do?”

Separate utilisation from value

Utilisation describes how much of a defined capacity has been booked or used. It can help with scheduling, but it does not tell an owner whether a service makes a sufficient contribution after direct costs or whether the workload is sustainable. A person can have a full diary of low-contribution services, or a lighter diary that allows necessary follow-up and recovery time.

Pair a booking measure with other operating indicators, such as:

  1. Contribution per service: the amount remaining after the direct costs you have chosen to include. Define the measure and state what remains outside it.
  2. Paid hours compared with available service hours: include clean-down, consultation, training and other required duties in the roster model.
  3. Wages as a share of service revenue: compare on a consistent basis, and explain whether super, leave, contractors, owner labour and other costs are included.
  4. Completed work and rework: a fast appointment is not efficient if it creates a safety concern, a poor client outcome or an avoidable return visit.
  5. Roster fit: review late starts, overrun, breaks, short gaps, cancellations and demand by day before concluding that a team member is under-used.

These measures should start a conversation, not become a blunt staff ranking. Compare like with like: service type, experience, training stage, shift pattern, season, client mix and the quality of source data all affect the result.

Connect roster decisions to service pricing

A service price should reflect the offer, customer value, local positioning, time and resources needed to deliver it. A staff-cost estimate is one input. It is not a formula that sets a fair price by itself.

For a service you want to review, capture the amount actually received after discounts, the booked duration, observed appointment time, product usage, direct transaction costs and any relevant follow-up. Record how much of the employee’s time is spent on consultation, preparation and clean-down. Use a sample large enough to be useful and note the date range, because a single appointment can be unusual.

If contribution appears low, investigate the cause before raising prices or changing staffing. The service might be underpriced. The appointment duration could be too short. Product usage may be unrecorded. The service could be an intentional introduction to a higher-value relationship. Or customers might need clearer information so they book the right service. Each explanation calls for a different response.

Do not set a target wage percentage based on a generic online benchmark. A salon with a high product-retail share, a clinic with specialised practitioners, and a solo barber have different economics. Compare a business with its own history and stated goals, and ask a qualified adviser to interpret formal payroll and accounting treatment.

Check what a roster is designed to accomplish

Before removing hours, understand the purpose of each role and shift. A front-desk employee may reduce interruptions for practitioners and help clients feel welcomed. A senior therapist may coach newer staff, maintain quality or handle complex consultations. Those contributions may not appear as direct appointment revenue, but they can support capacity and consistency.

Map the weekly work by role. Include open and close routines, client care, appointments, stock, cleaning, maintenance, staff training, breaks, follow-up and administration. Ask employees what regularly takes more time than planned. A repeated delay may be a system problem, an unrealistic booking duration, a supply issue, or a training need rather than individual under-performance.

Then test the roster against actual demand. Compare bookings, enquiries, cancellations, walk-ins if relevant, seasonality and the time required to deliver different service types. A simple heat map by day and time can reveal where the team is short, where capacity is unused and where the business may need a different service pattern. Treat each observation as evidence to investigate rather than a reason for an immediate cut.

Keep the denominator consistent

An hourly ratio is only useful when its denominator is defined the same way each time. “Available hours” might mean contracted paid hours, rostered hours, hours the employee is present, or hours set aside for client-facing work. Those are different measures. Choose the one that answers the decision in front of you, label it plainly and avoid switching definitions between months.

For example, a comparison of service revenue per rostered hour should use rostered hours for every period. If you instead compare revenue per booked appointment hour, that may help assess booking patterns, but it does not describe the full labour cost of the shift. Keep the two views separate. A spreadsheet can show paid time, non-client duties, service-available time and completed appointment time in adjacent rows, making the difference visible without pretending one figure explains everything.

Make the same choice about revenue. If the measure is service revenue, decide whether it includes discounts, refunds, package redemptions or GST and apply the same basis consistently. Retail sales can be relevant to the business, but combining them with service revenue may obscure the performance of a particular service line. State what is included beside the figure so a later reader does not have to guess.

Separate employee costs from the owner’s time

Small businesses often mix employee labour with the owner’s unpaid or irregularly paid work. That can make one service appear more profitable than it would be if the business had to pay someone else to perform all the required tasks. It can also make a role look expensive when the owner’s own client work is not counted on the same basis.

Keep the categories distinct. Record employee payroll costs using verified employment settings. Separately estimate the owner time spent delivering the service, preparing the space, answering enquiries, managing follow-up and overseeing the team. The owner’s estimate is a planning assumption, not payroll advice. It can still reveal whether a service depends on hidden effort that would become a capacity issue as demand grows.

This distinction is especially useful when comparing options such as hiring an employee, bringing in a contractor or changing the owner’s schedule. Compare the actual responsibilities, legal arrangements, supervision, continuity and quality implications alongside the money. An hourly comparison alone may miss the time spent recruiting, coordinating, checking work or managing client expectations.

Use a review period that reflects the work

One week may not show a normal pattern. A local event, public holiday, new starter, annual leave, school break, promotion or supplier delay can distort the picture. Select a period that captures the business’s normal operating cycle, explain any unusual conditions and revisit the result when the season changes. If the available records are incomplete, name the gap rather than filling it with false precision.

The purpose is not to create a perfect model. It is to make the assumptions clear enough that an owner and team can challenge them. If two people produce different results, the disagreement often points to a useful question: are they counting the same hours, service types and costs? Resolve the definition before acting on the number.

A practical cost review in four weeks

Week one: check the source. Confirm the applicable award or agreement, classifications, current rates, employment types and payroll settings. Save the official source and date checked. Ask a payroll professional about anything that is not clear.

Week two: map the work. For a small sample of shifts, record paid hours, service-available hours and the non-client duties that keep the business running. Ask the team whether the record misses important work. Do not collect unnecessary client personal information.

Week three: build a transparent estimate. Separate ordinary pay, super, penalties, allowances and other costs. Show formulas, assumptions, exclusions and the period used. If a cost changes by employee, role or state, keep that difference visible rather than averaging it away.

Week four: choose one test. You might adjust an appointment duration, improve how product use is recorded, move a task to a quieter period, or review one service price. Agree on a measure and a review date with the relevant people. Keep safety, customer experience, team workload and legal obligations in view.

Common mistakes to avoid

The first is treating the award rate as the complete cost of an hour. Another is applying one blanket percentage to every employee without checking the underlying pay, super and state obligations. A third is dividing all employment costs by client-facing hours but describing the result as the employee’s hourly wage; it is a business planning ratio, not a pay rate.

Owners can also overreact to a single month. A quiet period, a new employee’s training, annual leave, or a major local event can change the mix. Compare periods carefully and retain the context behind the figures. If an estimate affects wages, hours, role design, safety or contract terms, consult the relevant qualified professional before making a change.

Finally, avoid using a single wage metric to pressure people into rushing. The right operating model allows enough time to provide the agreed service, clean and prepare the space, record required information and take breaks. A saving that creates complaints, rework, injuries or staff turnover may not be a saving at all.

What matters most

The cost of a staff hour is a management view built from verified pay conditions, employer obligations and the way the business uses paid time. Keep each layer visible, use a consistent method, and separate estimates from formal payroll calculations. Then look at the whole service system: what clients pay, what work is required, how the team delivers it, and what the business can sustain.
