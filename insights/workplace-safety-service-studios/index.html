Workplace Health and Safety for Australian Clinics and Salons

Build a practical safety routine by identifying hazards, consulting staff and checking controls in your clinic.

Salons, barbershops, beauty rooms and tattoo studios combine customer service with physical work, equipment, chemicals, cleaning, electrical devices and often long periods on foot. Safety risks can be easy to overlook when the team is busy and the same routine happens every day. A small, regular safety process helps owners and workers notice problems before an injury or disruption occurs.

Australian work health and safety laws and regulators vary by jurisdiction and circumstances. This guide provides general operational ideas, not legal or clinical advice. Check current requirements with the relevant state or territory regulator and a qualified safety professional. Employers, workers and other duty holders have responsibilities; the correct duties depend on the work and relationship involved.

Identify the hazards in the actual workplace

Walk through the work as a practitioner would. Include opening, customer consultation, service delivery, cleaning, storage, payment, waste handling, deliveries and closing. Look at slips, trips, awkward reach, repetitive movement, standing posture, sharp tools, heat, electricity, ventilation, product exposure, infection risks, aggression and emergency access. The hazard profile depends on the service and equipment.

Ask workers what nearly went wrong. A loose power cable, blocked exit, missing product label or recurring wrist discomfort may not appear in a formal incident report, but could signal a pattern. Include casual and part-time workers in the conversation because they may notice different parts of the workflow. Consider cleaners, contractors, delivery workers and customers who enter the site.

Record the hazard, who may be affected, what currently controls it, the person responsible and the review date. A photo can help document a physical condition if it does not expose private customer information. Keep the register easy to update and avoid collecting unnecessary personal or health details. An operational log should support risk reduction rather than become a drawer of untouched forms.

Assess the risk and choose controls

Consider how likely an incident is, how serious it could be and how often people are exposed. A small spill beside a busy walkway may deserve immediate attention even if it seems minor. A low-probability electrical fault can still have serious consequences. Use the relevant regulator’s guidance and competent advice where the risk is complex, technical or involves hazardous substances.

Use the hierarchy of controls: remove the hazard where possible, substitute a safer option, isolate people from it, use engineering controls, set administrative procedures and use personal protective equipment where required. PPE is important for some work, but it should not be the only control if a more reliable solution is available. The correct choice depends on the hazard and applicable standards.

For example, repeated product spills might be reduced through appropriate dispensing equipment, safe storage and prompt cleaning. Poor workstation height may need equipment adjustment and a different task setup, not just a reminder to “sit straight.” A sharps hazard requires safe equipment, handling and disposal practices consistent with the service and official guidance. Never improvise a technical safety control from an online checklist.

Manage products and equipment carefully

Keep product labels and safety information available, and store items according to manufacturer instructions. Train the team in safe use, mixing, ventilation, spill response and disposal where relevant. Check that containers are clearly identified and not reused in a way that removes essential warnings. If instructions are missing or a product is damaged, stop and obtain supplier or safety advice before use.

Create a routine for inspecting electrical equipment, cords, guards, plugs and work surfaces. Remove damaged equipment from service and make repair responsibility clear. Use qualified tradespeople for electrical work. Keep pathways and exits clear, position tools so they do not create trip or cut hazards, and verify that equipment is suitable for the environment and task.

For beauty, hair and tattoo services, follow relevant professional, infection-control, licensing and product requirements. These can differ by activity and local jurisdiction. The business should confirm which rules apply rather than assuming that one state’s guidance covers the whole country. Seek a qualified specialist to assess sterilisation, ventilation, chemical exposure or infection-control requirements where needed.

Reduce physical strain and repetitive injury

Practitioners may repeat similar hand, shoulder, neck and back movements for long periods. Workstation design, chair height, equipment weight, lighting and task rotation can influence comfort. Ask staff to identify which positions or tasks create strain and consider adjustments. The best option should be tested with the people using the equipment, not selected from appearance alone.

Build variety into work where practical. Change tasks, take suitable breaks, keep frequently used items within comfortable reach and schedule setup time so practitioners do not rush. Consult workers about appointment lengths and service sequences. A schedule that leaves no time to clean, reset and move safely may create pressure and increase physical or cognitive fatigue.

Do not treat discomfort as a personal failure or ask a worker to push through pain. Encourage early reporting and seek appropriate health or ergonomic advice. Adjustments should preserve safe service and reasonable access for customers. If a person needs medical or return-to-work support, use qualified professionals and follow applicable privacy and employment processes.

Prepare for incidents and emergencies

Make emergency exits, first aid, emergency contacts and site instructions easy to locate. Decide who calls emergency services, who supports customers, who accounts for people and who contacts management. Practise the plan, including a scenario when the usual manager is absent. Keep an accessible copy for workers and review it after a layout or staffing change.

Record incidents and near misses promptly, in line with applicable rules and privacy requirements. Note what happened, the hazard, immediate actions, people notified and follow-up owner. Avoid blame language. The purpose is to understand the conditions that allowed the event and decide whether controls need to change. Serious incidents may have specific notification obligations; contact the relevant regulator and obtain professional advice.

A customer fainting, a chemical spill, a fire alarm or a power failure calls for different responses. Staff should know their training limits and when to stop work. Do not ask an untrained employee to manage a dangerous situation. Identify the emergency services, landlord, utility and equipment contacts the business may need. Keep this information available when phones or cloud systems are unavailable.

Involve workers and keep training current

Consult workers when identifying hazards, selecting controls and changing processes. People doing the work understand the practical details and can warn when a proposed solution will fail during a full day. Consultation is more effective when it happens before a decision is final and when the business explains what changed after receiving feedback.

Provide training matched to the actual task. Demonstrate safe equipment use, cleaning, product handling, incident reporting and emergency procedures. Confirm understanding through practice and keep a record where appropriate. A policy in a folder does not prove that a new worker knows how to perform a task safely.

Review training when a new product, tool, room layout or service is introduced. A casual worker or contractor may need a site-specific induction. Explain who controls the workplace and what coordination is needed with other businesses or building management. Get advice about duty-holder responsibilities when multiple parties control different parts of the workplace.

Make the routine practical for a small team

Use a short weekly visual check for obvious issues: walkways, exits, spills, equipment condition, storage, first-aid supplies and signage. Assign a named person and a route for urgent repair. A more detailed risk review can happen periodically and after a change, complaint, incident or near miss. The interval should reflect the work and risk.

Make reporting easy. A worker should be able to tell the owner about a hazard without filling out a long report during a busy service. Capture enough information to act and complete the record later if appropriate. Tell the team what happened after they raised an issue. Follow-through builds confidence that reporting matters.

Use a safety meeting as an operational check, not a lecture. Review one recent hazard, one change in procedure and any open actions. Keep notes of decisions, responsible people and due dates. If specialist assessment is required, pause the risky activity where appropriate until the risk is controlled. Do not rely on a small business’s limited experience for technical assessments beyond its competence.

A 30-day safety review

In week one, walk through the studio during a normal working day and list hazards across setup, service, cleaning and closing. Ask each worker for one recurring concern. In week two, identify urgent controls and assign an owner. Check current guidance from the local regulator and engage qualified advice for complex risks.

In week three, update one practical procedure and demonstrate it to the team. Check equipment, storage and emergency information. In week four, review whether the change was followed and whether it reduced the risk without creating another one. Record the result, update the hazard list and set the next review date.

Review cleaning and chemical handling routines

Cleaning and product handling should follow the manufacturer’s instructions and applicable safety rules. Keep products in suitable containers with required labels and safety information. Train staff to recognise exposure symptoms, manage a spill and find help. Do not mix chemicals unless instructions explicitly permit it. Keep incompatible products apart and ensure ventilation suits the task and room.

Check how often the routine occurs and whether the schedule allows it to be done properly. A room reset that is routinely compressed between appointments can lead to shortcuts. Adjust booking gaps, equipment placement or task allocation rather than relying only on staff reminders. Ask practitioners whether a product irritates them or whether a container is difficult to use safely. Escalate health concerns to an appropriate professional and handle personal information confidentially.

Waste and sharps disposal can have specific requirements. Confirm the correct containers, collection provider, storage and documentation for the services performed and local rules. Do not put a sharp or potentially hazardous item into ordinary waste because the approved container is full. Identify who checks supplies and who contacts the collection provider. The business should have a safe alternative while waiting for a replacement.

Check customer access and physical environment

The customer journey is part of the workplace environment. Check entrances, floors, lighting, stairs, waiting areas, accessible paths and privacy. A wet floor near the basin or a narrow route around equipment can affect customers as well as staff. Make hazards visible and address them promptly. Consider children, older customers and people who may need assistance, while respecting their independence and privacy.

Review manual handling of deliveries, chairs, equipment and product cases. Use suitable trolleys or team lifts where appropriate, store heavy items at safer heights and keep routes clear. Do not assume that a smaller business has no manual-handling risk because it has fewer employees. Repeated light loads can still cause strain over time.

Consider aggression, harassment and lone work. Decide how employees can request help, who responds and how an incident is recorded. Appointment-only work in a closed room may create different risks from an open barbershop. A simple check-in, panic process or clear customer policy may help, but select controls after assessing the actual work. Obtain specialist advice where threats or violence are a foreseeable risk.

Close the loop on hazards

A hazard report should lead to a decision. Set a review date, identify the person responsible and note whether the action reduced the risk. If the business cannot fix an issue immediately, use an interim control or stop the task until it is safe. Communicate the status to workers who may be exposed. A list with no owner gives the appearance of management without reducing danger.

Look at patterns across near misses, maintenance calls, discomfort reports and customer feedback. A recurring problem may indicate the process or equipment needs redesign. Review incidents with workers and protect sensitive details. Do not use a low incident count as proof that the workplace is safe; staff may not know how to report near misses or may fear blame.

Use a competent safety professional to assess unfamiliar or high-risk work. Verify qualifications, insurance, scope and follow-up responsibilities. For state-based obligations, use the regulator responsible for the business’s location and activity. National summaries can help orient an owner, but local requirements and codes may differ. Keep the source and date of the guidance used for important decisions.\n## Use the right source for local requirements

Australia has model work health and safety laws, but adoption and details can vary between jurisdictions. Identify the regulator for the place where the work occurs and check its current guidance. A business operating across states may need to confirm different requirements for each site. Do not rely on a national summary to answer a local licensing or notification question.

Ask a safety adviser to explain which duties apply to the business and which records should be retained. Confirm the adviser’s qualifications, scope and insurance. If the work involves hazardous chemicals, biological risks, electrical equipment or complex ventilation, obtain a competent assessment rather than relying on a generic online article. Keep the advice and date so it can be reviewed when circumstances change.

Review the safety system after a change

A new service, product, practitioner, room layout, booking pattern or piece of equipment can create new hazards. Before introducing the change, ask what could go wrong, who may be affected, how the risk will be controlled and whether training is needed. Update purchasing and cleaning instructions. Give workers a chance to test the arrangement and report practical problems before the business treats it as standard.

After implementation, observe the actual task. A control may look good on paper but be hard to use during a busy appointment. Check whether the team follows it, whether equipment fits the work and whether any new hazard appeared. Document changes and assign follow-up. If the risk cannot be adequately controlled, stop the task and seek expert advice.

A periodic review should also consider fatigue, workload and business pressures. An unrealistic appointment schedule can lead to rushed setup, skipped breaks or incomplete cleaning. Discuss workload with staff and consider how booking durations, staffing and room use affect safe work. Safety improvements can support productivity by preventing rework and disruption, but the business should never promise that every hazard can be eliminated.

Verify the plan through observation

After training, observe the task at a normal pace and during a busier period if appropriate. Look for whether the worker can find safety information, reach equipment without strain, dispose of waste correctly and stop when something is unsafe. Ask what makes the safe method difficult. Do not treat one successful demonstration as proof that every future condition is controlled.

Keep actions small and dated. A hazard log can record discovery date, interim control, final action, responsible person, verification date and remaining risk. Review overdue actions with the owner and explain why a control is delayed. If the risk remains unacceptable, pause the activity and obtain competent advice. A practical log makes unfinished risks visible instead of burying them in meeting notes.

Workers should know how to report a concern and receive feedback about the response. Where the business has several workers, create an appropriate way for them to raise issues and participate in decisions. Protect private information and avoid discouraging reporting through blame or targets. A positive reporting culture helps the owner learn about near misses before they become injuries.

What matters most

Safety is a daily operating responsibility. Identify hazards in the real workflow, consult the people doing the work, select suitable controls and verify that those controls are used. A small business still needs a process that matches its risks.

Use current state or territory regulator guidance, manufacturer instructions and competent professional advice. Keep emergency information accessible, report near misses and revisit controls when services or equipment change. Never rely on generic online guidance to settle a specialised safety question.
