A small service business may rely on a booking system, point-of-sale terminal, email account, cloud files, social media and a few personal devices. If one account is taken over or a laptop is lost, the impact can spread quickly: appointments are inaccessible, customer information is exposed, payments are delayed and the owner spends hours trying to restore normal work.

Cyber security does not begin with buying the most expensive software. It begins with knowing what matters, limiting access, protecting accounts, updating devices, keeping recoverable backups and deciding what to do if something goes wrong. This guide is a practical starting point, not a guarantee against attack or specialist security advice. Follow current Australian Cyber Security Centre guidance and seek professional help for complex systems or an incident.

## Map the systems that keep the business running

List the tools that store information or help deliver the service: booking and client management, payment provider, accounting software, email, website hosting, social media, cloud storage, staff scheduling, supplier portals, Wi-Fi, laptops, tablets and phones. Record who owns each account, who can access it, what data it contains, how it is recovered and where support can be reached. Avoid putting passwords in the inventory.

Identify the few services that would cause the most disruption if unavailable. A barber may be able to keep cutting hair for a short time without online booking but struggle without payment access. A tattoo business may need consultation records and artwork approvals. A beauty studio may need room schedules and practitioner information. A salon may depend on client colour notes. The business should determine which information is essential and whether sensitive details are being collected unnecessarily.

Draw a simple map of how data moves. An online booking may send a notification to email, create an appointment in a calendar, populate a customer record and produce a payment. Each connection adds convenience and another point to review. Ask which integrations are enabled, what access they have and how to revoke permission. Remove tools no longer used and accounts belonging to former contractors or staff.

## Protect email and important accounts first

Email is often the recovery path for other accounts. If an attacker controls the business email, they may reset passwords, impersonate the owner, intercept supplier invoices or send fraudulent payment instructions. Protect the email account with a strong, unique password and multi-factor authentication or passkeys where supported. Keep recovery phone numbers and backup codes current and stored securely.

Use a password manager to create and store unique passwords. Reusing a password across booking, email and social accounts means one breach can expose several services. Avoid sending passwords in plain text, writing them on a shared counter, or using a team-wide password without an approved sharing method. Give individual accounts to each worker so access can be removed when their role ends and activity can be attributed appropriately.

Turn on multi-factor authentication for email, finance, booking, cloud storage, social media and administrator accounts. The Australian Cyber Security Centre provides guidance on fundamental controls such as MFA, passkeys, unique passwords and software updates. Make sure more than one authorised person can recover critical business accounts where appropriate, but do not share recovery codes casually. Test account recovery before a real emergency.

## Keep devices and software up to date

Updates can close security weaknesses. Enable automatic updates for operating systems, browsers, phones, tablets, booking applications and routers when possible. If a device is too old to receive security updates, plan a replacement or remove it from sensitive work. An unsupported tablet used at the front desk can be a weak point even when the booking software itself is well maintained.

Install applications from trusted sources, review permissions and remove tools no longer needed. A photo editing app does not necessarily need access to contacts or location. Browser extensions can read sensitive information, so keep the number small and check who publishes them. Ask staff not to install unapproved software on business devices. A written one-page rule is often enough for a small team.

Secure Wi-Fi with a current router, strong administrator credentials and appropriate encryption. Change default passwords and separate a guest network from devices that access business records where your equipment supports it. Keep the router software current and understand who can reset it. If the business handles payment-card data, follow the requirements of the payment provider and obtain qualified technical advice rather than trying to design a compliant payment environment alone.

## Back up files and practise restoration

A backup is useful only if the business can restore it. Identify important records and files, then choose a backup approach that fits their source systems and recovery needs. Some cloud services provide version history or recycling, but synchronisation alone may copy a deletion or malicious change everywhere. Check the provider’s retention and restoration features rather than assuming that files are safely backed up.

Keep at least one recoverable copy protected from routine account access or accidental deletion. The exact approach depends on the system, data and risk. Avoid storing the only backup beside the computer it is meant to protect. If possible, restrict who can delete backup history and use multi-factor authentication for backup accounts. Document how to reach support and what identity checks are needed.

Schedule a test restore. Choose a sample file, restore it to a safe location and verify that it opens. For a booking platform, ask the provider what happens if the owner account is unavailable and what data export is available. Keep a secure copy of essential business information only when allowed and necessary. A business should know how it would operate if a key system were unavailable for a day or longer.

## Protect customer, staff and payment information

Collect only the information needed to provide the service. A booking form may need contact details and an appointment choice, but should not collect unnecessary personal history. Health or treatment information requires extra care and may involve legal and professional obligations. Store it only in an appropriate system with restricted access, clear purpose and retention rules. Do not put client details into public AI tools, personal notes apps or general chat without an approved privacy and security review.

Lock screens when leaving a device and position displays so customer information is not visible to other clients. Use separate staff logins, review permissions regularly and disable access promptly when a worker leaves. Limit exports and avoid sending appointment lists to personal email. A business can still provide convenient service without giving every team member unrestricted access to all records.

For payment safety, never ask customers to send card numbers or security codes through email or messaging. Use a reputable payment provider and follow its instructions. Verify unexpected changes to supplier bank details using a known phone number, not contact details in the suspicious message. Establish who can approve transfers and require a second check for unusual amounts. A short pause can prevent an urgent-looking invoice scam from becoming an expensive mistake.

## Train the team to recognise common scams

Scam messages often create urgency: an overdue bill, a password reset, an account suspension or a delivery problem. They may imitate a known supplier, booking platform or government agency. Check the sender address and destination of links, but remember that these can be forged. When in doubt, navigate to the known website or contact the supplier using a verified number already on file.

Give staff a simple reporting route. They should be able to say, “I clicked a link” or “I gave a code” without fear of blame. Early reporting gives the owner more options to change passwords, revoke sessions, contact a provider or monitor accounts. If a worker receives a suspicious message, ask them not to forward it to clients or enter credentials into the linked page.

Use brief, practical training rather than an annual technical lecture. Demonstrate how to report a suspicious email, verify a payment change, lock a screen and notify the owner of a lost device. Repeat when systems change or a new person joins. A small business can improve security through routine behaviour even without a dedicated IT department.

## Create a simple incident plan

Write down who makes decisions, who contacts the booking provider, bank, insurer, accountant, IT support and affected customers, and where emergency contact details are stored. Keep a printed or offline copy in a secure place. Include steps for lost devices, suspected account takeover, fraudulent payment instructions, website outage and accidental disclosure. The first response differs by incident, so use qualified guidance for the situation.

If an account is suspected to be compromised, use a clean, trusted device to change its password and revoke sessions where possible. Contact the service provider using an official channel. Speak to the bank promptly about suspicious payments. Preserve relevant evidence such as message headers, timestamps and transaction details. Do not wipe a device or delete messages before asking a specialist whether evidence is needed.

Australian businesses may have obligations to notify individuals or regulators depending on the data, incident and legal coverage. Do not assume every small business has identical duties. Seek legal and privacy advice, and follow official government guidance. The Australian Cyber Security Centre and business.gov.au provide current support and resources; in an active incident, act promptly through the appropriate official channels.

## A practical 30-day cyber security reset

In week one, list systems and account owners, identify critical accounts and remove access that is no longer needed. In week two, turn on multi-factor authentication for email and finance accounts, replace reused passwords with unique ones and store recovery information securely. If the owner cannot recover an important account, resolve that before adding new tools.

In week three, confirm automatic updates, check router access and review device locks. Identify important business records and verify backup or provider recovery options. Test a restore using a non-sensitive sample. In week four, write a one-page incident contact plan and spend 15 minutes showing staff how to report a suspicious message and verify payment changes.

Do not make every change in a way that locks the owner out. Keep an approved recovery route and test it. If the business cannot safely configure a setting, ask a reputable IT professional. Check whether the Australian Small Business Cyber Resilience Service or other support is currently available and whether the business meets its eligibility conditions before relying on it.

## Review cloud providers and connected services

A cloud vendor can be a critical business dependency. Check whether the vendor offers multi-factor authentication, individual user accounts, activity history, data export, account recovery, backup information, breach contact and support access. Understand where support requests go and whether support staff may access records. Read current terms and ask the vendor a direct question if the contract does not explain an important control.

Review every connection authorized to a business account. Booking, payment, accounting, email marketing and social tools may each receive permissions. Remove unused integrations and limit access to the minimum required. If a supplier requests a new connection, check its publisher and purpose through a trusted route. Do not approve a permission prompt simply because it appears during a busy shift.

Keep an inventory of renewal dates and account owners. The business should be able to reach the provider even if the person who set up the account is away. Store support IDs, recovery codes and backup details securely. If an administrator leaves, transfer ownership using the vendor’s approved process and confirm that personal payment methods or private email addresses are removed.

## Secure remote work and lost devices

If an owner checks bookings from home, use a secure device and trusted network. Avoid public Wi-Fi for sensitive account administration where a safer option exists. Keep operating systems current and lock devices when unattended. A stolen phone should not automatically expose email and booking details; use a screen lock, device encryption where supported, remote locking and a clear reporting route.

Decide in advance what happens when a device is lost. Who can revoke its sessions, change key passwords, contact the phone provider and inform the booking vendor? Keep the device identifier and provider contact available without exposing account credentials. If the lost device contained customer data or remained logged into a service, seek appropriate privacy and security advice promptly.

Do not assume personal devices are harmless because they are used only for a few minutes. Staff may receive booking alerts, screenshots or customer phone numbers. Establish whether personal devices are allowed, which approved apps can be used and how business data should be removed when a person leaves. Keep the policy proportionate to the actual risk and explain it clearly.

## Practise a short incident scenario

Choose one realistic event, such as a fake invoice requesting new bank details, a locked email account or a missing front-desk tablet. Spend 20 minutes asking what the first person should do, who is notified, which accounts can be secured and how the business would keep serving customers safely. Write down the gaps and assign an owner to close them.

Use the exercise to test contacts, not just written plans. Call the provider support number, check that the owner can access backup codes and confirm the insurer’s incident contact. Do not simulate a real attack on live systems. If a specialist is needed, ask them to create a safe exercise with clear permission and boundaries.

After an incident, review what happened without blaming the person who reported it. A no-blame response encourages earlier reporting. Identify whether a control failed, a message looked unusually convincing, a recovery path was missing or staff did not know who to contact. Update training and controls. The ACSC and business.gov.au resources can guide preparation, but a serious event may need immediate specialist support.\n## Make security ownership visible

Assign an owner for each critical account, even if that owner is the business proprietor. Record the backup contact, recovery method and provider support route. A small business may not have a dedicated IT manager, but someone must know which account can restore bookings and how to reach the vendor. Review ownership when an employee, contractor or technology provider changes.

Create a leaver checklist covering email, booking, payment, shared files, door codes, social accounts, marketing platforms and devices. Remove or adjust access on the person’s last working day and transfer business-owned content through the approved system. Do not keep a former worker’s account active simply because its password is convenient. If a contractor set up a website or cloud account, confirm the business controls the administrator and billing details.

Document who approves urgent payments and changes to supplier bank details. A second person can verify high-risk changes through a known phone number. If the business is too small for a second approver, define a short delay and independent check with the supplier. The rule should be practical enough to follow under time pressure. A written process helps staff resist a convincing message that says an owner is unavailable and asks them to act immediately.
## What matters most

Protect email and finance accounts, use MFA and unique passwords, update software, restrict data access, test backups and plan how to get help. These basics reduce common risks and make recovery more practical. They do not eliminate all threats, and a larger system or incident may need specialist support.

Make the process fit the business: a one-person beauty room and a multi-chair salon have different people and systems, but both need clear ownership and a recovery route. Review access when staff or suppliers change, keep customer data out of unapproved tools and practise restoring a file. The current Australian Cyber Security Centre guidance should be the reference point as products and threats change.
